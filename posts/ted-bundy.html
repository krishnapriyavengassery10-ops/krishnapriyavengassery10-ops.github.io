The Ted Bundy Case

krishnapriyavengas · Sep 1 · 3 min read

Introduction

Ted Bundy is one of the most well-known names in American crime history. He was a law student, had worked as a volunteer for the Republican party, and by most accounts came across as charming and well-spoken. That makes his case even more unsettling, since this same man confessed to killing at least 30 women across seven states in the 1970s, and many investigators believe the actual number was higher. His case ended up being important for how police approach criminal profiling, how they treat victims, and how forensic evidence is used in court.

Background

Bundy was born Theodore Robert Cowell on November 24, 1946, in Burlington, Vermont. He was raised mostly in Tacoma, Washington, attended the University of Washington, and worked briefly in Republican politics. By outward appearances, he seemed like a normal, even promising young man. That image is part of why his case is so unsettling — it's also likely part of why his victims trusted him in the first place. One of his tactics was faking an injury, like wearing a cast or using crutches, so people (usually young women) would offer to help him, and he would attack them.

The Murders and Investigation

Bundy's known killings began in the early-to-mid 1970s in the Pacific Northwest and later extended to Utah, Colorado, and eventually Florida. Most of his victims were young women with long hair parted down the middle, a detail that later helped investigators connect cases that had initially seemed unrelated.

Because Bundy moved between states, and because the FBI did not yet have a strong system for tracking violent crimes across state lines, law enforcement in different places did not immediately realize they were looking for the same person. Detectives in Washington, Utah, and Colorado were separately investigating disappearances with strikingly similar details — young women last seen near college campuses or recreational areas — before the cases were connected. Bundy's case went on to shape how the FBI's Behavioral Science Unit approached criminal profiling.

He was first arrested in August 1975 in Utah, after a survivor named Carol DaRonch identified him in a police lineup following an attempted abduction. He was convicted of kidnapping for that incident.

Colorado later indicted him for the 1975 murder of Caryn Campbell. While awaiting trial there, Bundy escaped custody twice in 1977. The first time, he jumped from a second-story courthouse window in Aspen and was recaptured within days. The second time, on New Year's Eve, he escaped through a gap in the ceiling of his jail cell. That second escape allowed him to reach Florida, where he committed his final and most heavily documented murders at the Chi Omega sorority house at Florida State University on January 15, 1978, killing Margaret Bowman and Lisa Levy and severely injuring two other women.

Capture and Trial

Bundy was arrested for the final time on February 15, 1978, in Pensacola, Florida, after an officer became suspicious of a car he was driving, which turned out to be stolen. Bundy initially gave a false name before his real identity was confirmed a few days later. His Florida trials were among the first in the country to be televised nationally. Prosecutors also used bite-mark evidence to connect him to the Chi Omega murders, a technique that was considered cutting-edge at the time but has since been widely criticized by experts as unreliable.

Bundy also served as his own co-counsel for part of the trial, which gave the public an unusually close look at his manipulative tendencies, particularly during cross-examinations.

Execution and Legacy

Bundy was executed in the electric chair at Florida State Prison on January 24, 1989. Shortly before his execution, he confessed to FBI Special Agent Bill Hagmaier and other investigators that he had killed 30 people across seven states between 1973 and 1978. Britannica notes that some sources put his confessed count at 36, and investigators have long suspected the real total was higher, though it will likely never be confirmed.
