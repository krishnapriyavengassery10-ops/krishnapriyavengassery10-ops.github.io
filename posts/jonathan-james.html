The Boy Who Hacked NASA: Jonathan James

krishnapriyavengas · Sep 13 · 3 min read

Introduction

Jonathan Joseph James was born on December 12, 1983, and grew up in an upper-middle-class family in Miami-Dade, Florida. His father was a programmer and so computers were a natural part of his life. Jonathan taught himself Unix and C while most of his classmates were still struggling with dial-up connections and his contemporaries described him as an inquisitive, but shy teen who did not consider illegal downloading as something wrong.

Breaking Into the Pentagon and NASA

In 1999, James decided on penetrating the firewalls of two of the most protected computer systems in the USA. First, he targeted the Defense Threat Reduction Agency, the Pentagon wing that tracks nuclear, biological and chemical weapons and created a backdoor for himself in one of their servers. Quietly again, James used a network sniffer to capture more than 3000 messages passing through the government's machines and the usernames and passwords of many military staff.

Not stopping there, he decided to infiltrate the servers of the National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) and stole valuable software worth $1.7 million. This software was used to regulate the temperature and humidity of the International Space Station. The hack caused NASA to shut down all systems for three weeks before they could confirm that their systems were not compromised, costing them tens of thousands of dollars.

According to James himself, he did not commit the crimes for financial gain, nor to boast of his skills, as he was just playing a game. He was not concerned with being caught because, in his own words, he did not think he was doing anything wrong.

The Raid

On January 26, 2000, the boy's house was raided by the Defense officials, NASA security guards and local police. Six months later Jonathan James was indicted and agreed to plead guilty to two charges of juvenile delinquency to avoid serving time in an adult prison. On September 2000, he was sentenced to house arrest for seven months with probation until his eighteenth birthday.

In the meantime, his case became the first of its kind as Jonathan James became the first-ever juvenile to be imprisoned for a cybercrime and so legislatures were forced to grapple with the dilemma of what to do with teens who could commit such crimes against the country's security systems.

The Years After

Released in 2001, James was still a troubled and depressed teen with a federal prison record that traveled with him wherever he went in the closely-knit computer hacker world. According to his family, he was still struggling with his low self-esteem before taking his own life at age 24 on May 18, 2008, at his Pinecrest, Florida, home.

According to his suicide note, James confessed that he committed the TJX hack, but he was frustrated that authorities did not believe him and that he had lost all faith in the system. In addition to losing control of his life, he indicated that this was the only way he could regain his power.

The TJX hack involved the theft of tens of millions of customer card numbers in what was then the largest data theft in America. While authorities investigating the TJX breach traced the hacking ring to Albert Gonzalez, James was questioned by the Secret Service, but to no avail.

The Legacy

Jonathan James's life was a classic story of a genius turned villain whose life ended in tragedy. His case is the stuff of legend among true crime enthusiasts as it offers insight into the psychology of not only a hacker but also of a justice system that was ill-prepared to deal with such cases. His story changed many laws concerning cybercrimes in the United States, but it will always be a pertinent reminder that every criminal case is not a morality play.

The tragedy of James's life is that while he committed shocking crimes, he did not deserve the harsh punishment that the system wanted to give him. True crime buffs will always wonder if a significant portion of his pain was caused by the system, which took away his life's pleasures, rather than the TJX breach itself.
