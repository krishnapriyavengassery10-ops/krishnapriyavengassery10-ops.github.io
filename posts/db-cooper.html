The D.B. Cooper Hijacking

krishnapriyavengas · Sep 19 · 4 min read

Introduction

On the night before Thanksgiving in 1971, a man bought a plane ticket under a fake name, hijacked the flight, collected $200,000 in ransom, and then jumped out of the back of the plane with a parachute. He was never seen again. To this day, nobody knows who he actually was.

The Hijacking

On November 24, 1971, a man calling himself Dan Cooper bought a one-way ticket for Northwest Orient Airlines Flight 305, going from Portland, Oregon to Seattle, Washington. He looked like any other business traveler — dark suit, tie, sunglasses, calm and polite the whole time. Once the plane was in the air, he passed a note to a flight attendant saying he had a bomb in his briefcase. To prove it, he opened the case just enough for her to see a mess of wires and red cylinders.

His demand was $200,000 in cash and four parachutes, to be ready when the plane landed in Seattle. The airline agreed to everything. Once they landed, Cooper let all 36 passengers go in exchange for the money and parachutes, but he kept a small crew on board and ordered the plane back into the sky, this time headed toward Mexico City. He also told the pilots to fly low and slow, with the landing gear down — which, it turned out, was exactly what he needed for what came next.

The Jump

Somewhere over the forests of southwest Washington, in the middle of a rainstorm, Cooper opened the rear stairway of the plane — something only the Boeing 727 could do mid-flight — strapped the money to himself, and jumped. Nobody saw him do it. Nobody knows exactly where he landed. And nobody has seen him since.

The Investigation

The FBI's search for Cooper, called NORJAK internally, turned into one of the longest investigations in the bureau's history. Thousands of tips came in from the public, mostly because Cooper had somehow become a folk hero to a lot of people — which honestly just made things harder, since agents had to chase down leads that went nowhere.

Search teams spent years combing the forest where they believed he came down. Nothing was found — no parachute, no body, no sign that anyone had landed there at all. Over the decades, more than a thousand people were looked at as possible suspects, including a former paratrooper and even a Northwest Airlines employee, but nobody was ever confirmed.

The Money That Was Found

In 1980, a young boy digging along the Columbia River in Washington found a rotting bundle of cash — $5,800 in decaying $20 bills. The serial numbers matched Cooper's ransom money exactly. It's still the only physical evidence from the case that was ever recovered, and instead of solving anything, it just raised more questions. Nobody has ever explained how that money ended up there.

Is It Still Unsolved?

Yes. The FBI officially stopped actively investigating in 2016, saying they had exhausted every credible lead, but the case was never technically closed. It remains the only unsolved hijacking in the history of American commercial aviation.

So in a strange way, Cooper's fifteen minutes on that plane changed air travel for everyone who flew after him — his hijacking is a big part of why airports now have metal detectors and mandatory baggage checks, and why planes can no longer lower their stairs mid-flight. Not bad for someone whose real name nobody has ever confirmed.
